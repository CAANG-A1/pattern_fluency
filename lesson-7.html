One Number Tells a Story

Before This Lesson
A number on a screen is just a count.
→
After This Lesson
A single number, placed correctly, answers the most important question in the room.

Welcome Back

Every lesson so far has been about charts. Today we talk about something different: a single number. Not a chart. Not a table. One number on a page.

In Power BI, this is called a card visual. And in the right context, it's the most powerful thing on the page.

Think about the last briefing you sat in. At some point, someone said a number and the room shifted. "We're at 73% readiness." "12 people haven't completed mandatory training." Those weren't charts. Those were numbers. And they changed the conversation.

Before We Open Power BI

The Most Important Number

What is the single most important number you could pull from your data? Not the most interesting chart — the number that, if someone saw it, would immediately understand something important. Write it down before you open anything.

Step 1 — Build a Card Visual

Building the Card

Click on an empty area of the canvas. In the Visualizations panel, find the card icon — it looks like a square with a number inside. Click it.

Drag the column that contains your number into the card. Look at what appears. Is it the number you expected?

Step 2 — Control What the Card Shows

Choosing the Calculation

Click the card. In the Fields panel, look for the dropdown arrow next to your field. Click it. Choose the right option:

Count — how many rows have a value. Count (Distinct) — how many unique values exist. Sum — add all values together. Average — the mean.

The Meaning Check

Before you move on, read the card out loud as a sentence: "There are [number] [thing]." Does that sentence answer your question? If yes, the card is right. If no, change the calculation type until the sentence is true. Do this every single time.

Step 3 — The Three-Piece View

On a clean canvas, build three cards side by side. Each card answers a different question about your data. Examples: Total headcount. People who completed training. People who have not completed training.

Look at the three cards together. Someone can walk in, see those three numbers, and understand the situation in under 10 seconds. That's the goal of a card visual — not detail, not depth, but immediate understanding.

Drill #5

▶ The Sentence Drill

Build a card. Read it as a sentence. Write the sentence down. Delete the card.

Build a different card using a different column. Read it as a sentence. Write it. Delete it.

Build a third card. Apply a filter. Read the new sentence. Delete it. Repeat three times.

You're training yourself to always ask: "What does this number actually say?"

Reflection

  1. 01What is the most important single number in your data?
  2. 02Did the number Power BI showed match what you expected? Why or why not?
  3. 03What's the difference between Count and Count (Distinct)?
  4. 04What does the three-card view communicate that a chart can't?
  5. 05What label would make each card immediately understandable to someone who didn't build it?

Challenge

Build a summary view that could replace a verbal briefing. Three to five cards, each answering one specific question, each with a label that makes it self-explanatory. Show it to one real person. Ask: "What does this tell you?" Write down exactly what they say. Did they understand it correctly?

Teach Back

Explain what a card visual is and when to use it. Explain the meaning check and how to do it. Explain why one number is sometimes more powerful than a chart. Explain how three cards together can replace a verbal briefing.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Trusting the default number without checking

Power BI will make an assumption about Count vs Sum. It won't always be right. Always do the meaning check.

Mistake 2 — Building cards without labels

"47" means nothing to someone who didn't build the report. "47 personnel with incomplete training" means everything. Labels are meaning, not decoration.

Mistake 3 — Using too many cards

Eight cards is visual noise. Three to five is the effective range. If you need more, ask whether some should be a chart instead.

Mistake 4 — Forgetting that cards can be filtered

A card with no filter answers a broad question. A card with a filter answers a focused question. Know which one you've built.

Homework

Build a three-card summary of your most important work data. Each card answers a question your unit actually cares about. Each card has a label anyone could read and understand. Save this — you'll build on it in Lesson 6.

Lesson Summary

Before this lesson, a number was just a count.

After this lesson, a number is an answer — and placing that answer where the right person will see it is a design decision, not an afterthought.
