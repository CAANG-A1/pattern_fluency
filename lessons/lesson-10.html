Patterns Over Time

Before This Lesson
Data is a snapshot. It shows me where things stand right now.
→
After This Lesson
Data over time is a story. It shows where things are going — and how we got here.

Welcome Back

Last lesson you connected two tables and answered a question that neither could answer alone. Today you add a new dimension: time.

A single report tells you where things stand right now. A trend tells you where things are going — and whether they're getting better or worse.

The difference between a snapshot and a story is a date column.

Pattern Review

Pattern #3 (Read the Shape) taught you to look for what's in a spreadsheet before building anything. Today, the most important thing to look for is a date column. If it exists, you can tell a story over time. If it doesn't, you're limited to snapshots. Know which one you have before you build.

Before We Open Power BI

Look at the data you've been working with.

Does it have a date column? A month column? A fiscal year column?

If yes — you can build a trend. If no — you'll need to find or add one before this lesson makes sense with your data.

For today, we'll build a simple dataset with dates so you can practice the pattern regardless of what your real data looks like.

Step 2 — Build the Trend Line

The Line Chart

Click a blank area of the canvas. Find the line chart icon in the Visualizations panel. Click it.

Place Month in the X-axis box.

Place Readiness in the Y-axis box.

Look at the canvas. You should see a line rising and falling across time.

Now drag Unit to the Legend box.

Two lines appear — one per unit. Now you can compare trends side by side.

If the X-axis Shows Numbers Instead of Months

Power BI may have read your Month column as a number instead of a date. Click the Month column in the field list on the right. Look for a data type indicator. If it shows a number icon instead of a calendar icon, right-click the column name → Change data type → Date or Text. Reimport if needed.

What a Trend Reveals That a Snapshot Hides

A snapshot of June shows Unit 129 at 94% readiness. Impressive. But the trend shows that Unit 144 has been climbing steadily while Unit 129 peaked in April and dipped before recovering. The snapshot answers "where are we?" The trend answers "where are we going and how did we get here?"

Those are completely different questions.

Step 3 — Read the Trend

Look at your line chart. Answer these questions out loud before reading on:

Which unit is trending up? Which is trending down? Which had the sharpest change? At what point did the lines cross?

The goal is not to memorize numbers. The goal is to see a story.

The Trend Question

A bar chart asks: who has the most right now?

A line chart asks: where is this going?

Both questions matter. Neither replaces the other. Use bar charts for comparisons. Use line charts for stories over time.

What You Just Learned

You may think you just learned how to build a line chart. You didn't. You learned your tenth Pattern Fluency pattern. When you want to know where something is going — not just where it is — you need a date column and a line chart. The trend is the story. The snapshot is just one chapter.

Pattern #10

Read the Trend

Question: Where is this going — and how did we get here?

Recipe:

  • Look for a date column in your data. If it doesn't exist, ask why and find one.
  • Place the date column in the X-axis.
  • Place your measure in the Y-axis.
  • Use a line chart — not a bar chart.
  • Add a category to the Legend to compare multiple trends.
  • Read the story: what's rising, what's falling, where did the lines cross?

Example: Month → X-axis. Readiness → Y-axis. Unit → Legend. Story: Unit 144 is catching up to Unit 129.

Drill #10

▶ The Trend Reading Drill

Build the line chart with both units.

Look at it for 30 seconds. Write down one sentence describing the trend for each unit.

Remove Unit from the Legend. State the new question the chart answers.

Add Unit back. Switch to a bar chart. State what you lose.

Switch back to a line chart. State what you regain.

Repeat three times. Every time — write the sentence describing the trend before you change anything.

Find the date column. Put it on the X-axis. Use a line chart. Read the story, not just the numbers.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Using a bar chart when you mean a line chart

Bar charts compare values at a single point in time. Line charts show change over time. If your X-axis is a date, you almost always want a line chart. The bar chart will give you bars for each date — readable, but not the trend.

Mistake 2 — Missing the date column

If your data doesn't have a date column, you can't build a trend. This is a data shape problem (Pattern #3). Ask where the date information is — it may be in a filename, a tab name, or a separate tracking system.

Mistake 3 — Reading individual points instead of the line

The value of a trend is the direction, not the individual data points. Train yourself to read the slope first, then the individual values. "It's going up" matters more than "it's at 88 this month."

Mistake 4 — Too many lines on one chart

More than four or five lines on one chart becomes unreadable. If you have many categories, use Pattern #4 (Filter = New Question) to focus on the most important two or three.
