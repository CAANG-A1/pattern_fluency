Welcome Back

So far, you have learned five patterns.

Pattern #1: Group & Count helped you count records by category.

Pattern #2: Change the Lens helped you choose the visual that explains the answer most clearly.

Pattern #3: Read the Shape helped you understand what your data can and cannot answer.

Pattern #4: Filter = New Question helped you focus broad questions into specific questions.

Pattern #5: One Number Answer helped you identify the single number that matters most.

By now, your canvas probably has charts, cards, filters, and labels scattered across it.

Some things may be different sizes.

Some labels may still say things like "Count of Name" or "Sum of Budget_FY24_Final_v3."

Some visuals may be easy for you to understand because you built them, but confusing to someone else.

The Shift

Today we clean it up.

But not because reports should look pretty.

We clean it up because a report that's hard to read doesn't get read.

Design is not decoration.

Design is communication.

Before We Open Power BI

Your Audience

Think about the specific person who will read your report.

Not "leadership in general."

Someone specific.

Write down:

  • Who is this person?
  • What question are they trying to answer?
  • How much time will they spend on this report?
  • What would confuse them?

Keep these answers in front of you.

Every design decision today is made for this person.

Step 1 — Fix Your Labels

Open Power BI.

Open the report you've been building.

Look at every label on every visual.

Do any of them say things like:

  • Count of Name
  • Sum of Budget_FY24_Final_v3
  • Training Complete by Unit

Those labels may be accurate, but they are not always meaningful to someone who did not build the report.

Changing a Title

Click on a chart.

Find the Format panel. It may look like a paint roller or formatting icon on the right side of the screen.

Find the title section.

Change the title to a plain-English question or statement.

Instead of: Count of Name by Unit

Write: Headcount by Unit

Or: How many people are in each unit?

Do this for every visual on your page.

The Label Test

A good title tells the reader what question the visual answers.

If the reader has to ask, "What am I looking at?" the label has failed.

Step 2 — Align and Arrange

The Newspaper Rule

Most people read left to right, top to bottom.

Put the most important information at the top left.

Put supporting detail below or to the right.

Your cards — the key numbers — usually go at the top.

Your charts — the detail — usually go below.

What's most important should be easiest to find.

Resize your visuals so they fit clearly on the page.

No overlapping.

No visuals hanging off the edge.

Use consistent spacing between elements.

Ask yourself:

If someone had 30 seconds, where would their eyes go first?

Step 3 — Use Color With Intention

Look at the colors on your canvas.

Does anything jump out?

Good — that thing should be important.

If nothing jumps out, everything has equal visual weight.

That's a problem.

If everything is colorful, nothing is important.

If one thing is colorful, that thing matters.

Use Color as a Signal

Pick the most important visual on your page.

Give it a slightly stronger color, border, or emphasis.

Leave everything else more neutral.

The goal is not to decorate the report.

The goal is to guide the reader's attention.

Step 4 — Add a Slicer

Building a Slicer

Click on an empty area of the canvas.

In the Visualizations panel, find the slicer icon. It usually looks like a funnel.

Click it.

Drag a category column into the slicer — something your audience would want to filter by.

Good first slicer choices include:

  • Unit
  • Rank
  • Status
  • Training Complete

Avoid using Name for your first slicer.

Slicers work best when they group information rather than isolate one person at a time.

Click one of the values.

Watch everything on the page shift.

Your audience now controls the focus.

You're not just building a report.

You're building a tool.

What You Just Learned

You may think you just learned formatting.

You didn't.

You learned how to design for the reader.

Labels tell the reader what question is being answered.

Layout tells the reader where to look first.

Color tells the reader what matters most.

Slicers let the reader control the focus.

Pattern #6

Design for the Reader

Question: Can the intended audience understand this without me explaining it?

Recipe:

  • Identify the reader.
  • Rewrite labels in plain English.
  • Put the most important information first.
  • Use color to guide attention.
  • Add slicers when the reader needs control.
  • Test with a real person.

Formatting is not decoration.

Formatting is meaning.

Drill #6

▶ The Audience Drill

Add a slicer to your page.

Click each value.

Watch the page respond.

Click multiple values at once.

Clear the slicer.

Watch the page return to its full view.

Now step back and ask:

Would my specific reader know what to do with this?

Repeat this three times.

Do it as your audience would.

You're learning what it feels like to use this report, not just to build it.

Reflection

  • 01What label change made the biggest difference?
  • 02What did you remove from the canvas — and why?
  • 03What color did you use to draw attention to the most important visual?
  • 04How does the slicer change the experience of using the report?
  • 05If your specific person had 30 seconds with this report, what would they understand?

Challenge

Show your report to one real person.

Do not explain it.

Just let them look at it.

After 30 seconds, ask:

"What does this tell you?"

Then ask:

"What's confusing?"

Fix the one thing that confused them most.

Redesign is part of design.

Teach Back

Explain why labels matter more than people think.

Explain what a slicer is and how it's different from a filter.

Explain how you decide where to put things on the canvas.

Explain how you use color to show what matters most.

Then explain Pattern #6 in plain language:

Design the report so the reader knows what matters without you standing there to explain it.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Keeping the default labels

Changing labels takes two minutes and makes the report ten times more readable. Always change them.

Mistake 2 — Making everything colorful

When every chart has a different color scheme, nothing stands out. Use color sparingly. One accent color for what matters most. Neutral for everything else.

Mistake 3 — Building for yourself instead of your audience

You know what everything means because you built it. Read every label as if you've never seen this data before. If you wouldn't understand it cold, neither will they.

Mistake 4 — Never testing with a real person

You cannot proofread your own report effectively. Show it to someone. Listen to what confuses them. That confusion is your design problem to solve.

Mistake 5 — Adding slicers nobody needs

A slicer should answer a real audience need. If nobody needs to filter by that field, it is clutter.
