One Number Tells a Story

Before This Lesson
A number on a screen is just a count.
→
After This Lesson
A single number, placed correctly, answers the most important question in the room.

Welcome Back

So far, you've learned four patterns.

Pattern #1: Group & Count helped you answer how many records are in each category.

Pattern #2: Change the Lens helped you choose the visual that explains the answer most clearly.

Pattern #3: Read the Shape helped you understand what your data can and cannot answer.

Pattern #4: Filter = New Question helped you focus broad questions into specific questions.

Every lesson so far has leaned heavily on charts.

Today we talk about something different: a single number.

Not a chart. Not a table. One number on a page.

The Surprise

Sometimes the best chart is no chart at all.

Sometimes the clearest answer is one number, placed where the audience can see it immediately.

In Power BI, this is called a card visual.

And in the right context, it can be the most powerful thing on the page.

Think about the last briefing you sat in. At some point, someone said a number and the room shifted.

"We're at 73% readiness."

"12 people haven't completed mandatory training."

"We have 4 overdue actions."

Those weren't charts.

Those were numbers.

And they changed the conversation.

Before We Open Power BI

The Most Important Number

What is the single most important number you could pull from your data?

Not the most interesting chart.

Not the most detailed table.

The number that, if someone saw it, would immediately understand something important.

Write it down before you open anything.

Step 1 — Build a Card Visual

Building the Card

Click on an empty area of the canvas.

In the Visualizations panel, find the card icon. It usually looks like a square with a number inside.

Click it.

Drag the column that contains your number into the card.

Look at what appears.

Is it the number you expected?

Power BI Makes Assumptions

Power BI will automatically choose a calculation.

Sometimes it chooses Count.

Sometimes it chooses Sum.

Sometimes it chooses something else.

Always verify that the card is showing the number you intended.

If You Only Have Category Columns

That's okay.

You can still build useful cards.

If your data has names, statuses, units, ranks, or training categories, use Count.

Example: placing Name in a card can show the total number of people.

Example: placing Status in a card can count how many records have a status.

Step 2 — Control What the Card Shows

Choosing the Calculation

Click the card.

In the Fields or Visualizations area, look for the dropdown arrow next to your field.

Click it.

Choose the option that matches your question:

  • Count — how many rows have a value.
  • Count (Distinct) — how many unique values exist.
  • Sum — add all values together.
  • Average — calculate the mean.
The Meaning Check

Before you move on, read the card out loud as a sentence:

"There are [number] [thing]."

Does that sentence answer your question?

If yes, the card is right.

If no, change the calculation type until the sentence is true.

Do this every single time.

What You Just Learned

You may think you just learned how to build a card visual.

You didn't.

You learned how to identify the single answer that matters most.

Sometimes a chart is unnecessary.

Sometimes the answer is a number.

Pattern #5

One Number Answer

Question: What is the one number the audience needs first?

Recipe:

  • Identify the most important question.
  • Build a card visual.
  • Choose the correct calculation.
  • Read the card as a sentence.
  • Label it so someone else understands it instantly.

One number is not decoration.

One number is an answer.

Step 3 — The Three-Piece View

On a clean canvas, build three cards side by side.

Each card should answer a different question about your data.

Examples:

  • Total headcount
  • People who completed training
  • People who have not completed training

Look at the three cards together.

Someone should be able to walk in, see those three numbers, and understand the situation in under 10 seconds.

That's the goal of a card visual.

Not detail.

Not depth.

Immediate understanding.

Drill #5

▶ The Sentence Drill

Build a card.

Read it as a sentence.

Write the sentence down.

Delete the card.

Build a different card using a different column.

Read it as a sentence.

Write it down.

Delete it.

Build a third card.

Apply a filter.

Read the new sentence.

Delete it.

Repeat the sequence three times.

You're training yourself to always ask:

"What does this number actually say?"

Reflection

  • 02Did the number Power BI showed match what you expected? Why or why not?
  • 03What's the difference between Count and Count (Distinct)?
  • 04What does the three-card view communicate that a chart can't?
  • 05What label would make each card immediately understandable to someone who didn't build it?

Challenge

Build a summary view that could replace a verbal briefing.

Use three to five cards.

Each card should answer one specific question.

Each card should have a label that makes it self-explanatory.

Show it to one real person.

Ask:

"What does this tell you?"

Write down exactly what they say.

Did they understand it correctly?

Teach Back

Explain what a card visual is and when to use it.

Explain the meaning check and how to do it.

Explain why one number is sometimes more powerful than a chart.

Explain how three cards together can replace a verbal briefing.

Then explain Pattern #5 in plain language:

Find the number that answers the question. Make sure the number means what you think it means. Label it clearly.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Trusting the default number without checking

Power BI will make an assumption about Count vs Sum. It won't always be right. Always do the meaning check.

Mistake 2 — Building cards without labels

"47" means nothing to someone who didn't build the report. "47 personnel with incomplete training" means everything. Labels are meaning, not decoration.

Mistake 3 — Using too many cards

Eight cards is visual noise. Three to five is the effective range. If you need more, ask whether some should be a chart instead.

Mistake 4 — Forgetting that cards can be filtered

A card with no filter answers a broad question. A card with a filter answers a focused question. Know which one you've built.

Mistake 5 — Using a card when the audience needs comparison

If the real question is "which category is bigger?" use a chart. If the real question is "what is the number?" use a card.
