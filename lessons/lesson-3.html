Some questions are possible. Some questions are not. The difference is the shape of your data.

Same information. Different shape.

Which spreadsheet can tell you who specifically hasn't completed training?

Write your answer before reading on.

The Observation

Spreadsheet A can answer "Who hasn't completed training?" because it has names.

Spreadsheet B can only answer "How many?" because the names are gone.

The shape changed.

And when the shape changed, some questions became impossible.

The shape of your data determines what questions it can answer.

The Two Types of Columns

Every column in a spreadsheet is one of two things.

A category column groups things together. It contains words or labels. Examples: Unit, Rank, Status, Training Complete. Power BI uses these to group and filter.

A number column measures things. It contains quantities. Examples: Count, Budget, Days, Score. Power BI uses these to count, sum, and average.

Watch Out

A number is not always a number column.

Unit numbers like "129" and "144" are categories.

They label. They don't measure.

If you wouldn't add two values together and expect something meaningful, it's a category column.

Step 1 — Label Your Columns

Open any spreadsheet you work with.

For each column, write:

  • C for category
  • N for number

Do this before opening Power BI.

Take your time.

Step 2 — Predict Before You Build

Before opening Power BI, write:

"If I place [column name] in the X-axis, I predict Power BI will group the data by ____."

Do this for three columns.

Then import your spreadsheet and test your predictions.

Drill #3

▶ The Prediction Drill

Click a blank area of the canvas and select a bar chart.

Place a category column such as Unit, Rank, or Training Complete into the X-axis.

Place a record-identifying column such as Name into the Y-axis.

Say out loud:

"Power BI grouped by ___ and counted ___."

Delete the chart.

Repeat using a different category column.

Do this three times.

If the Chart Does Not Show Anything

Check the field boxes before you assume something is broken.

  1. Is a chart selected?
  2. Is your category field in the X-axis?
  3. Is Name or another record-identifying field in the Y-axis?

The Question the Data Can't Answer

Does It Feel Right?

Look at your spreadsheet.

Are there questions you want to answer that this data can't support?

What's missing?

A column that doesn't exist?

A category too broad?

A number never recorded?

That gap between what you want to know and what your data can tell you — we'll learn to close it in Volume 2.

For now, just notice it.

What You Just Learned

You may think this lesson was about categories and numbers.

It wasn't.

It was about understanding the limits of your data.

Some questions are possible.

Some questions are impossible.

The difference is the shape of the data itself.

Pattern #3

Read the Shape

Question: What can this data answer, and what can it not answer?

Recipe:

  • Identify category columns.
  • Identify number columns.
  • Identify missing information.
  • List the questions the data can answer.
  • List the questions the data cannot answer.
  • Determine why.

Before building a report, learn to read the shape.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Assuming all numbers are number columns

If a number labels or identifies something, it's a category. If it measures something, it's a number column.

Mistake 2 — Overlooking obvious columns

Name and ID columns seem unimportant but are often what make individual-level questions answerable. Remove them and you lose the ability to ask "who."

Mistake 3 — Thinking more columns means better data

A spreadsheet with 40 columns and no clear structure is harder to work with than one with 5 well-designed columns.

Mistake 4 — Skipping the prediction step

Predicting before you drag is where your brain learns the rule. Skip it and you'll relearn it from experience every time.
