Design for One Person

Before This Lesson
A good report is accurate and easy to read.
→
After This Lesson
A good report makes the right decision obvious to the person who needs to make it.

Welcome Back

You can connect tables, read trends, create measures, and drill into detail. You have real analytical capability now.

Today is not about a new technical skill. Today is about what all those skills are for.

Pattern #6 (Design for the Reader) taught you to design for someone who didn't build the report. In Volume 1, that meant labels, layout, color, and slicers.

Volume 2's version of that lesson is harder: design for a specific decision.

Not a person who will read the report. A person who will make a choice because of the report.

Pattern Review

Pattern #6 asked: can the reader understand this without me explaining it? Today's pattern asks something harder: can the reader make the right decision because of this report — without me being in the room?

That is the difference between a report and a decision support tool.

Before We Open Power BI

The Decision First

Write down a specific decision your unit needs to make in the next 30 days.

Examples: "Which unit gets priority scheduling for the remedial training?" or "Should we request additional funding for the supply shortfall?"

Name the person who makes that decision. Not the person who reads the report — the person who makes the call.

What does that person need to see to make the right decision? Write it down before touching Power BI.

Step 1 — Map the Decision to the Data

Look at the decision you wrote down. Now ask:

What data supports this decision? What would make the answer obvious? What would cause the decision-maker to ask a follow-up question?

The Decision Blueprint

Write three things before building anything:

1. The decision: What will this person decide after seeing the report?

2. The must-see number: What is the one number that most directly informs the decision?

3. The supporting context: What two or three additional visuals give the decision-maker confidence?

That blueprint is your report design. Everything else gets left off.

Step 2 — Build the Decision-First Report

Layout for Decisions

Open Power BI. Build your report following this layout:

Top row: Cards showing the must-see numbers. These answer "what's the situation?" in under 10 seconds.

Middle row: One or two charts that provide context — a trend (Pattern #10) or a comparison (Pattern #1).

Bottom row (optional): A drill-down chart (Pattern #12) for decision-makers who want to verify the detail.

Add a slicer if the decision-maker needs to focus on a specific unit, time period, or category.

Add a title that states the decision, not just the data: "Training Readiness: Which Units Need Priority Scheduling?"

The Hardest Part

The hardest part of this lesson is leaving things off.

You now have measures, trend charts, connected tables, and drill-down hierarchies. It's tempting to show all of it.

Don't.

Every visual that doesn't directly support the decision is noise that slows the decision-maker down.

The test: if a visual were removed, would the decision-maker be less able to make the right call? If no — remove it.

What You Just Learned

You may think you just learned how to design a better report. You didn't. You learned your thirteenth Pattern Fluency pattern. When the goal is a decision — not just understanding — every visual must earn its place by directly informing that decision. Anything that doesn't make the decision easier gets removed.

Pattern #13

Design for the Decision

Question: What does this person need to see to make the right call?

Recipe:

  • Name the decision — not the report topic. "Should we prioritize Unit 129 for remedial training?" not "Training Report."
  • Name the must-see number — the one that most directly answers the decision.
  • Name the supporting context — the two or three visuals that provide confidence.
  • Remove everything else.
  • Title the report as the decision, not the data.
  • Test: show it to someone. If they can make the right call without you explaining anything, it works.

Example: Decision: which unit gets priority scheduling? Must-see: incomplete training count by unit (card + bar chart). Context: trend showing trajectory. Remove: every other chart.

Drill #13

▶ The Removal Drill

Open a report you've already built — from this volume or Volume 1.

For every visual on the page, ask: "Does removing this make the decision harder?"

If no — remove it.

Count how many visuals you removed.

Show the reduced report to one person. Ask: "Can you make the decision this is designed for?"

If yes — the removals were correct. If no — add back only what's needed.

Reflection

  • 01What was the specific decision your report is designed to support?
  • 02What is the must-see number — and why is it the most important?
  • 03What did you remove from the report — and was it hard to remove?
  • 04Could the decision-maker make the right call without you in the room?
  • 05What's the difference between a report that informs and a report that decides?

Challenge

Show your decision-first report to the person who actually makes the decision it's designed for.

Do not explain it. Do not walk them through it. Just hand it to them.

After two minutes, ask: "What would you decide based on what you see?"

Did they make the right call? Did they ask for something the report doesn't show? What does that tell you?

Teach Back

Explain the difference between designing for a reader and designing for a decision.

Explain how you decide what to remove from a report.

Explain Pattern #13 in plain language:

Name the decision first. Find the must-see number. Add two visuals for context. Remove everything else. Title it as a decision, not a report.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Showing everything you know

A report that shows everything you know about the data is a data dump. A report that shows exactly what the decision-maker needs is a decision tool. They are not the same. Never confuse thoroughness with usefulness.

Mistake 2 — Naming the report after the data instead of the decision

"Unit Training Report" tells the reader what the data is. "Which units need priority scheduling?" tells the reader what to do with it. Titles that state the decision make reports more powerful.

Mistake 3 — Designing for your own curiosity

The measures, trends, and hierarchies you built are genuinely interesting. That doesn't mean they belong on this report. Design for the decision-maker's needs, not your analytical enthusiasm.

Mistake 4 — Not testing with the actual decision-maker

You cannot know whether a report supports a decision until you watch someone make it. Always test with the person who will actually use the report — not with yourself or a proxy.

Homework

Find a real decision your unit faces in the next month.

Build a report specifically designed to support that decision.

Show it to the decision-maker before the decision is made.

After the decision is made, ask: "Did the report help? What was missing?"

Write down the answer. You'll use it to improve the design.
