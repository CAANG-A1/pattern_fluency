Filters Change the Question

Before This Lesson
Filters remove data I don't want to see.
→
After This Lesson
Filters change the question I'm asking.

Welcome Back

In Lesson 1, you learned Pattern #1: Group & Count.

In Lesson 2, you learned Pattern #2: Change the Lens.

In Lesson 3, you learned Pattern #3: Read the Shape.

Today you're going to discover something that seems small but changes everything: filters.

Most people think filters hide data.

That's not wrong — but it's not the most useful way to think about them.

A filter changes the question.

Without a filter: "How many people are in each rank?"

With a filter: "How many people in the 129th Wing are in each rank?"

Same chart. Same data. Different question. Different answer.

Pattern Review

Pattern #1 helped you count records by category.

Pattern #2 helped you change the visual without changing the data.

Pattern #3 helped you understand what your data can and cannot answer.

Today, you'll discover how narrowing your focus changes the question itself.

Before We Open Power BI

Look at the question you brought from your Lesson 3 homework.

Now write a more specific version by adding one constraint.

Examples:

"Which rank has the most people?" becomes "Which rank has the most people in the 129th Wing?"

"How many completed training?" becomes "How many TSgts completed training?"

Write your more specific question down.

That's what we're answering with a filter today.

Step 1 — Build Your Base Chart

Open Power BI and load your data.

Build a bar chart using Pattern #1:

  1. Select a bar chart.
  2. Place a category column in the X-axis.
  3. Place Name, or another record-identifying field, in the Y-axis.

Make sure the chart answers your original broad question before applying any filters.

Step 2 — Add a Filter

Adding a Filter

Find the Filters panel on the right side of the screen.

Find the column that contains your constraint — the thing you want to focus on.

Drag it into the Filters panel.

A list of values appears.

Check the box next to the value you want to focus on.

Look at the chart.

What changed?

The Shift

Before the filter, your chart answered:

"All of our people — how are they distributed?"

After the filter, your chart answers:

"Just the people in [your filter] — how are they distributed?"

The chart looks similar.

But the question is completely different.

The filter does not remove data from your spreadsheet.

It focuses the lens.

What You Just Learned

You may think you just learned how to use a filter.

You didn't.

You learned your fourth Pattern Fluency pattern.

A filter is not just a way to hide data.

A filter is a way to ask a more specific question.

Pattern #4

Filter = New Question

Question: What happens when I focus on one part of the data?

Recipe:

  • Start with a broad question.
  • Build the broad chart.
  • Choose one field to focus on.
  • Apply the filter.
  • Restate the new, focused question.

Example: "How many people are in each rank?" becomes "How many people in the 129th Wing are in each rank?"

Step 3 — Try Different Values

Keep your chart.

Uncheck the value you selected.

Check a different value.

What changed?

Now check both values at once.

What changed again?

You just asked three different questions using the same chart.

Page-Level Filters

Filtering the Whole Page

Find the Filters panel.

Look for Filters on this page.

Drag a column into it.

Check a value.

Now look at every chart on your page — they all changed.

One filter. Every visual. Same focus.

This is how dashboards work.

Drill #4

▶ The Question Drill

Start with a clean chart.

Apply one filter.

State the question out loud.

Change the filter.

State the new question out loud.

Remove the filter.

State the original question out loud.

Repeat three times.

Each time you state the question out loud, you're connecting the filter to the meaning, not just the mechanics.

Spot the Pattern

Choose the Pattern Before You Click

For each scenario, decide which pattern you would use first.

  1. Your commander asks: Which unit has the most overdue training?
  2. Your audience asks: Are we looking at counts or percentages?
  3. You receive a new spreadsheet: You are not sure what questions it can answer.
  4. Your supervisor asks: What does this look like for only the 129th Wing?

Answer key:

  • Scenario 1 → Pattern #1: Group & Count
  • Scenario 2 → Pattern #2: Change the Lens
  • Scenario 3 → Pattern #3: Read the Shape
  • Scenario 4 → Pattern #4: Filter = New Question

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Filtering before knowing the question

Always write the question first. Then filter. Clicking into the filter panel without knowing what you're focusing on is just clicking.

Mistake 2 — Forgetting the filter is on

You apply a filter, build three more charts, share the report — and everything is scoped to a value no one else knows about. Always check your filters before sharing.

Mistake 3 — Filtering down to meaningless groups

If your filter results in two rows, ask whether the chart is still telling you something real. Small groups produce unstable patterns.

Mistake 4 — Using filters when you should use slicers

Filters require you to set them before sharing. In Lesson 6, you'll learn slicers — visible on-screen filters your audience controls themselves.
